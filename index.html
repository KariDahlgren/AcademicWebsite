Kari Dahlgren studies how people and communities navigate uncertain futures in the context of contested energy and industrial transitions in Australia.

Senior Lecturer in Anthropology, School of Social Sciences, Monash University

kari.dahlgren@monash.eduGoogle ScholarORCIDLinkedIn

Dr Kari Dahlgren is a social anthropologist and ethnographer whose research examines the social and ethical dimensions of energy production, consumption, and transformation in Australia. Her research brings together the anthropology of energy, futures anthropology, and the study of industrial transformation.

Her recently published monograph, Coal’s Final Void: Extracted Futures and the Everyday Making of the Climate Crisis (2025), draws from ethnographic research in Australian coal regions to trace the complex social, ethical, and political dynamics that sustain fossil fuel economies. It examines the contradictory ways communities, workers, and industry lobbyists navigate the anticipated end of coal, and theorises the “final void” as a site where extractive pasts and speculative futures are held in tension.

She has an ongoing program of research on energy futures, including collaborative projects with industry and policy partners that explore changing patterns of household energy demand and interdisciplinary scenario-building such as the Digital Energy Futures and Scenarios for Future Living projects, which integrate ethnographic research into energy system planning.

She also studies industrial decarbonisation in Australia, revealing how decarbonisation is unfolding through processes of addition and infrastructural layering, in which “old” and “new” systems coexist and intersect. This includes how extractive and fossil fuel industries, materialities, and logics, remain embedded within emerging low-carbon industries and imaginaries.

Her research is broadly concerned with how futures are anticipated and negotiated, and with the uneven effects that follow.

Research

Industrial decarbonisation and contested energy futures.

2025–2029 Hydrogen Hub Futures How the hydrogen hub model for industrial decarbonisation in Australia is imagined, contested, and lived. ARC DECRA Fellowship
Years
2025–2029
Funder
Australian Research Council, Discovery Early Career Researcher Award

The hydrogen hub model is envisioned to fast-track environmental approvals, draw on existing social licence from previous industrial workforces, and accelerate the scaling-up of hydrogen by co-locating supply with demand in heavy industry.

This project asks how imagining regions as 'hubs' produces forms of circular speculation, may end up deferring decarbonisation, and concentrates environmental and social impacts in particular communities, as well as how such visions enact and reproduce inequalities. The project is the centre of a broader research agenda on industrial decarbonisation and its social effects in Australia.

Field site: Whyalla, South Australia

An industrial city on the Upper Spencer Gulf, home to a steelworks and to one of South Australia's flagship hydrogen proposals.

Whyalla brings together many of the dynamics at the heart of the hydrogen hub model: a long-standing heavy industry and workforce, state-led investment in hydrogen production, power generation and storage, and the promise that co-locating hydrogen supply with industrial demand could help decarbonise steelmaking.

It is also a place where hydrogen futures have been announced, revised and deferred, making it a revealing site for understanding how hub visions are lived, contested and imagined by the communities expected to host them.

Questions the project brings to Whyalla
  • How does imagining a region as a hub shape local expectations of jobs, growth and decarbonisation?
  • How do hydrogen hub visions relate to longer histories of industrial transformation?
  • When do speculative hydrogen futures accelerate decarbonisation, and when do they defer it?

2014–2019 Precarious futures in Australian coal mining towns Studying those whose lives are entangled in the coal industry, and the everyday making of the climate crisis. Doctoral research, LSE
Years
2014–2019
Institution
London School of Economics (PhD, 2019)
Supervisors

Kari's doctoral research involved two years of in-depth participant observation in Australian coal mining communities. Rather than framing coal communities or lobbyists as either victims or villains, it explores how moral claims are mobilised in everyday life and how these shape energy futures.

The work contributes to debates on the Anthropocene and energy ethics, showing how climate change is lived not as an abstract crisis but as an ethical and moral problem entangled with people's livelihoods, identities and futures. By recognising how accusations and counter-accusations entrench polarisation, it points towards ways of engaging fossil-fuel communities that move beyond moralising.

The book from this research: Coal’s Final Void (Routledge, 2025)

Teaching

Notice360 is an immersive 360° video and virtual reality tool for teaching ethnographic observation.

Notice360 approximates the experience of being guided by a trained ethnographer through observations on campus, cultivating what anthropologist Anna Tsing calls “the arts of noticing” (2015). It is optimised for VR headsets but also runs on desktop and mobile devices, so it can be used in online tutorials and by students for whom headsets aren’t suitable.

There are two versions. The guided version uses annotations and voice-over to prompt students to notice what an ethnographer would and to ask critical questions of the environment around them. The unguided version approximates observing without that guidance, allowing the team to test how much guidance matters in observational training.

What students said

In the first trial, 68 students answered an optional survey. 88% found Notice360 more engaging than the average tutorial, and 23.5% said it was one of the most engaging tutorials they’d had.
